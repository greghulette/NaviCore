NaviCore · Production Reference

Setup & Configuration Walkthrough

Ten parts, filmed in dependency order — the order the work actually happens in, which is not the order the config tool's tab strip runs in.

10 parts ~81 minutes total Part 6 is the longest Part 5 is modular

Why this order. The tab strip reads General → Channels → Logical → Transmitter → WCB → HCR → MP3 → Maestro → WLED → Smoothing → Baud → Firmware. Filming it that way breaks three times: Firmware is last in the UI but first in reality, destinations must exist before anything can be mapped to them, and calibration is meaningless until the transmitter model is loaded.

The through-line. Parts 3–4 establish what controls exist. Part 5 establishes where commands can go. Part 6 connects the two. Everything before is prerequisite, everything after is payoff.

00

Before You Start

4 minNo software yet — bench and wiring only

Sets expectations and prevents the two wiring mistakes that make everything downstream mysterious.

  • Which board you have. NaviCore v2 PCB or WCB HW 3.2. This choice changes the GPIO pin map and it is the first thing you'll set in software.
  • The UART map. SBUS in on Serial1, local Maestro on S2, aux devices on S3/S4/S5, SBUS passthrough out on GPIO9.
  • The port-numbering trap. The same three aux ports are silkscreened Serial 1/2/3 on NaviCore v2 and Serial 3/4/5 on WCB 3.2. Say this out loud with both boards on camera.
  • Port capability differs. S3 is the hardware UART and goes to 115200. S4/S5 are bit-banged SoftwareSerial and cap at 57600. This decides where WLED can live.
  • What you need on the bench: transmitter, receiver, at least one Maestro, and a Chromium browser. Everything else — WCB boards, HCR, MP3 trigger, WLED — is optional and covered modularly in Part 5.

Film the failure

  • Show a device on S4 at 115200 not working, then the same device on S3 working.
  • Show what "no SBUS" looks like in the monitor, so viewers can recognize a dead receiver link later.
01

Flash & First Connect

5 minFirmware tab

First in reality, last in the tab strip. Nothing else can be demonstrated until the board answers.

  • Flash from the browser. The Firmware tab pulls the binary straight from github.com/greghulette/NaviCore/firmware. No Arduino IDE, no toolchain.
  • Version awareness. "Currently on board" populates from the board's PING reply; "Latest on GitHub" from the repo. Show the ↻ re-check.
  • Reassure about config loss. NVS is preserved on a normal update — only blank or partition-mismatched boards get a full flash. People will assume flashing wipes their work.
  • Connect over Web Serial. Port picker, what the right port looks like, what a successful connect looks like.
  • Reset to factory defaults on camera so every viewer is starting from the same state you are.

Film the failure

  • The port not appearing in the picker — and the driver fix.
  • Trying to connect in a non-Chromium browser and getting nothing.
  • A board that's connected but not answering PING, and how you tell the difference.
02

Transmitter Model

6 minTransmitter tab

Channel numbers mean nothing until the model exists. Calibration in Part 3 depends entirely on this.

  • Pick your model: FrSky Tandem X18, FrSky Twin X-Lite, or FrSky Twin X20.
  • Explain what the model actually drives: which transmitter graphic is shown, which controls appear during calibration, and the default channel assignment for each control.
  • Load the bundled model file onto the transmitter's SD card and select it, so the channel map matches what NaviCore expects.
  • X20 owners: the 3-axis gimbal option adds J5/J6 twist axes plus the two stick-click momentary buttons at matrix slots 19/20. Leave it off on a standard 2-axis X20.
  • Custom mixers still work. Calibration overrides per-control channels individually — the model only supplies starting points. Worth saying explicitly; it unblocks anyone with an existing setup.

Film the failure

  • Wrong model selected — the graphic doesn't match the transmitter in your hand, and channels land on the wrong controls.
  • 3-axis enabled on a 2-axis X20: phantom axes in the table that never move.
03

Foundations

10 minGeneralBaud RatesChannels

The densest part of the video. Everything here is a global that silently breaks later work if it's wrong.

  • Board / PCB. Set it, save, then reboot — pins are assigned at boot, so a save alone does nothing.
  • Tap window. 100–2000 ms, default 500. This is the multi-tap detection window and it directly shapes how Part 6 feels to use.
  • Telemetry rate. 1–20 Hz for the live channel monitor. Higher is smoother; lower frees mesh airtime.
  • SBUS output. Off by default. Only enable if you daisy-chain a downstream SBUS device — leaving it off saves the per-frame CPU of re-transmitting.
  • Baud rates. S2 for the local Maestro bus, then S3/S4/S5. One port = one device = one baud. Must match what the device is set to.
  • Matrix & mode. Which SBUS channel carries the multiplexed buttons (7 by default), and which switch — SA through SH — drives Mode 1/2/3. Show what "disabled" means: always Mode 1.
  • Debounce. 1 frame is right for a digital SBUS source and resolves taps roughly twice as fast. Raise to 2–4 only when driving from a physical resistor-ladder matrix. Applies on save, no reboot.
  • Calibrate against the live monitor. Per-button trigger values and labels, switch channel and 2/3-position type, then the analog axes — sliders LS/RS, pots S1/S2, sticks J1–J4 — including the reverse checkbox.

Film the failure

  • Change the board type, save, don't reboot — show nothing happening. Then reboot and show it work.
  • A baud mismatch on the Maestro bus: commands go out, servos don't move.
  • Two buttons with overlapping trigger values, and how the monitor reveals it.
04

Logical Buttons

5 minLogical Buttons tab

How you get more buttons than your transmitter physically has. Half of this happens on the transmitter, which is why people get stuck.

  • What they are: 15 extra buttons riding the matrix channel that don't appear anywhere on the transmitter graphic.
  • How they fire: mix a transmitter logical switch — or any spare source — onto the matrix channel, then set the value it outputs. When the matrix channel reaches that value within ±10, the logical button fires.
  • They are first-class. Same per-mode behaviour, same multi-tap, same action editor as a physical button. Nothing is second-tier about them.
  • Film the transmitter side. Build the mix on the transmitter on camera. This is the half that isn't in the config tool and it's where every stuck viewer will be.
  • Leave a trigger blank to disable that slot.

Film the failure

  • A logical button value that collides with a physical button's value — both fire, and it looks like a ghost press.
  • A value set correctly in the tool but never actually output by the transmitter mix — dead button, nothing in the monitor.
05

Destinations

14 minmodularWCBMaestroHCRMP3WLED

Nothing can be mapped to a destination that doesn't exist yet. Break these into five clips — most viewers don't own all five.

  • WCB Network — 5 min. MAC octets 2 and 3, password, quantity, device ID, and the WiFi channel that must match every board. Query an existing board with ?WCBM / ?WCBP / ?WCBQ / ?WCBCH. Explain that quantity is a pre-registered floor, not a limit — extra boards auto-join. Remote WCBs need firmware 6.2.0 or newer; check with ?version. Show the REBOOT PENDING badge and honour it: credentials are only read at boot. Close with the WCB Send test — unicast 1–20, or 0 to broadcast.
  • Maestro Locations — 4 min. Eight logical Maestros; buttons and knobs address "Maestro 1–8" and never care about the wiring. Every physical Maestro needs a unique Pololu device number (0–127) set in Maestro Control Center, matching the value here. Demonstrate importing a Maestro settings file — or dragging it onto a row — to pull in real per-channel endpoints and names, which then drive passthrough ranges and the timeline editor.
  • HCR — 2 min. One global destination, via Serial or via WCB. Every HCR action routes through it. Set once, save.
  • MP3 Trigger — 1.5 min. Serial means wired to this controller's aux ports; WCB means wired to another board and configured there with ?MP3,S<port>.
  • WLED — 2 min. One row per WLED with an ID from 1–9, addressed by actions as ;L<id>,VERB. Route to a local aux port or a remote WCB. WLED wants 115200, so realistically only S3 — tie this straight back to the port-capability point from Part 0. A bare ;L,VERB drives the lowest-id local WLED.

Film the failure

  • Save WCB credentials, skip the reboot, watch nothing join. Then reboot.
  • A WiFi channel mismatch between boards — everything looks configured, no peer ever appears.
  • Two Maestros sharing a device number: both respond to one command.
  • WLED on S4 at 115200 — garbage or silence. Move it to S3.
06

Mapping

18 minlongest partaction editorSmoothing

The reason anyone is watching. Everything before this was setup; this is where the droid starts doing things.

  • Open the action editor from a button and walk its structure before assigning anything.
  • Maestro actions in full: set servo position, send all servos home, run a script by subroutine, stop the script, and the speed and acceleration limits. Speed is 0–16383 and acceleration 0–255, with 0 meaning unlimited in both cases — the value everyone gets backwards. Positions are entered in µs.
  • The other action families: WCB commands (unicast and broadcast), HCR, MP3 trigger, WLED lighting, and raw serial. One real on-droid result for each.
  • Modes. Switch the mode tabs and map the same button differently per position. This is where the combinatorics become concrete.
  • Multi-tap. Single, double, triple — and the exclusive versus cumulative distinction, which needs a slow, deliberate demonstration. Tie it back to the tap window set in Part 3.
  • Switches get an action per position rather than per tap.
  • Knobs, sliders and joysticks drive continuous output, and each picks a smoothing profile in its editor.
  • Smoothing profiles. Per-Maestro, per-channel easing — really the Maestro's speed and acceleration limits behind a friendlier control. Slide right for more easing, far left for snappy. A Maestro "set active easing" switch action can swap profiles live, so the same servos glide or snap on demand. Enable the Maestro in the Maestro tab before its channels appear here.
  • Test without saving: Shift+Click a configured button to fire it.

Film the failure

  • Speed set to 0 expecting "stopped", getting full speed instead.
  • A double-tap that keeps registering as two singles because the tap window is too short.
  • Mapping to a Maestro that was never enabled in the Maestro tab.
07

Record & Replay

8 minRecordLibrary

The headline feature, and it only makes sense once viewers have controls mapped. Don't move it earlier.

  • What gets captured: everything the droid does over a bounded window — Maestro moves, HCR, WCB commands, MP3, aux serial — saved as a named clip.
  • Perform, then save. Record a real sequence live on the transmitter rather than clicking it together. That's the whole appeal.
  • Anti-snap. Servo positions are snapshotted at clip start and eased into on replay, so a clip never slams servos from wherever they happened to be.
  • The library. Name clips, rename them, build a set. Clips live on their own flash partition, so they don't compete with your config or OTA space.
  • Trigger a clip from a button, which closes the loop back to Part 6. Toggle works on any button; level and hold need a switch.

Film the failure

  • Replaying a clip that starts from a very different servo position, so viewers see the ease-in doing its job.
  • Trying to use a hold trigger on a momentary button.
08

Finishing

5 minWCBcheat sheet

The quality-of-life layer. Short, high-delight, and a good place to breathe after Part 7.

  • New-peer event. The passive alert is a cyan LED pulse plus a terminal line; you can also run actions when a board joins. Fires once per board after an 8-second boot grace, so your existing fleet doesn't set it off at power-on.
  • The cheat sheet. Generate it and walk what it contains — buttons by mode, switches, knobs, recorded sequences, and the interactive transmitter graphic you can tap for assignments.
  • Send it to your phone with the QR button, then show it working at the droid where you'd actually want it.
  • Back up. Export the config and say plainly why: it's the only copy that survives a board swap.

Film the failure

  • Nothing to break here — use the time for the phone demo instead.
09

Verify & Troubleshoot

6 minterminal

The most re-watched part of any setup video, because it's where people land when their build doesn't work.

  • Walk the whole chain once — transmitter, monitor, action, droid — naming each hop so viewers know where to look when a hop is dead.
  • Use the terminal as a diagnostic. Show what a healthy exchange looks like so an unhealthy one is recognizable.
  • Then the greatest hits, each reproduced live: REBOOT PENDING ignored, WiFi channel mismatch, duplicate Maestro device numbers, baud mismatch on an aux port, WLED on a bit-banged port, and a remote WCB running older than 6.2.0.
  • Close on the recovery path: reset to defaults, re-import your backup, and you're back where you started.

Film the failure

  • This entire part is the failure reel — budget time to stage each one properly rather than describing it.

Notes for the shoot

  • Film each part as its own session. A fluffed take then costs one part, not the whole run.
  • Publish twice from one shoot. A playlist of ten videos, plus the full concatenation as a single chaptered upload. Most viewers arrive at Part 6 from a search and never see the rest.
  • Show the failure states. Most setup tutorials only film the happy path and become useless the moment a viewer deviates. The amber boxes above are the highest-value footage in the whole project.
  • Screen-first, droid-second — the inverse of an overview video. Zoom the browser to about 150%; half your audience is watching on a phone at the bench.
  • Say every value out loud and put it on screen, so people can pause on it.
  • Don't silently cut the waiting. If discovery takes 30 seconds, say so — people panic when their run is slower than the video.
